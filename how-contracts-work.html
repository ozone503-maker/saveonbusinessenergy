How contracts work

A commercial electricity contract sets your energy rate, your term, and your obligations. The delivery charges on your bill are separate. Here is what is actually in one.

Your contract sets your energy rate and your term. The delivery charges are separate — and the end date is the number that matters most.

The three parts of your bill

Energy rate

The price the retail electric provider charges for the electricity itself. This is the number most people compare. It is not the whole bill.

TDU delivery charges

Your transmission and distribution utility delivers the power, maintains the lines, and handles outages. In much of DFW, that is Oncor. In some pockets, it is TNMP.

Those delivery charges are regulated and stay on your bill no matter which provider you choose.

Demand charges

For many commercial meters, peak demand (kW), not just kilowatt-hours, is a large part of the delivery bill. That is separate from the energy rate a provider quotes.

Fixed vs. variable

Fixed

A fixed-rate contract keeps the energy rate level through the term. A fixed rate gives you a known number for the term.

Variable

A variable rate can change with the market. A variable rate can move up or down.

What happens when the contract ends

The holdover rate

After a fixed contract expires, many businesses move to a month-to-month or holdover rate if they have not signed a new contract. Check your contract, because the new rate can be much higher.

Find the date

Your contract end date is on your bill or your contract.

Things to check before you sign

Power factor

Some utilities adjust billing demand when power factor is low. In Oncor territory, the tariff allows an adjustment when power factor falls below 95% lagging.

Tax status

Texas generally charges sales tax on business electricity, with exemptions in specific cases, for example electricity used in manufacturing when the predominant use qualifies.

Can you choose?

In competitive areas, you choose the REP. Retail choice for eligible Texas customers began January 1, 2002, after Senate Bill 7 (1999).

Many municipal utilities and electric cooperatives, including Garland and Denton, are not open to choice. Confirm from your bill or ESI ID, not from the city name alone.

Your ESI ID is the ID number for your electric service point. It is printed on your bill.

Sources: PUCT 2003 Scope of Competition in Electric Markets in Texas; Oncor Tariff for Retail Delivery Service, as summarized by Wire & Watt.

FAQ

How do I know if my address can choose a provider?

Check your bill or your ESI ID, not the city name alone. Many municipal utilities and electric cooperatives are not open to choice, even inside a city that mostly is.

What is an ESI ID?

Your ESI ID is the ID number for your electric service point. It is printed on your bill. It is the reliable way to confirm whether your address is open to retail choice.
