How Texas commercial electricity works

Energy broker

An energy broker does not sell you electricity. A broker shops your business account to retail electric providers, compares offers, and helps you sign a contract. Brokerage services are provided through USA Energy Partners.

Retail electric provider (REP)

The company you choose to supply your electricity in a deregulated area. We are not a retail electric provider.

TDU / Oncor charges

Your transmission and distribution utility (in much of DFW, that is Oncor; in some pockets, TNMP) delivers the power, maintains the lines, and handles outages. Those delivery charges are regulated and stay on your bill no matter which provider you choose.

Deregulated (retail choice)

In competitive areas, you choose the REP. Retail choice for eligible Texas customers began January 1, 2002, after Senate Bill 7 (1999). Many municipal utilities and electric cooperatives, including Garland and Denton, are not open to choice. Confirm from your bill or ESI ID, not from the city name alone.

Source: PUCT 2003 Scope of Competition in Electric Markets in Texas

Fixed vs. variable rate

A fixed-rate contract keeps the energy rate level through the term. A variable rate can change with the market. After a fixed contract expires, many businesses move to a month-to-month or holdover rate if they have not signed a new contract. Check your contract, because the new rate can be much higher.

Power Factor

Some utilities adjust billing demand when power factor is low. In Oncor territory, the tariff allows an adjustment when power factor falls below 95% lagging. We check whether this is quietly raising your bill.

Source: Oncor Tariff for Retail Delivery Service, as summarized by Wire & Watt.

Tax Status

Texas generally charges sales tax on business electricity, with exemptions in specific cases — for example, electricity used in manufacturing when the predominant use qualifies. We look for exemptions your business may be missing.

Sources: Comptroller Publication 94-124 and 34 TAC § 3.295.

Demand charges

For many commercial meters, peak demand (kW), not just kilowatt-hours, is a large part of the delivery bill. That is separate from the energy rate a provider quotes.

What is the average commercial electricity rate in Texas?

The U.S. Energy Information Administration's final 2025 average price to Texas commercial customers was 8.56 cents per kWh, compared with 13.37 cents for commercial customers nationwide and 15.40 cents for Texas residential customers. For September 2026, EIA's preliminary Texas commercial average was 8.96 cents per kWh. These are statewide averages across every kind of commercial customer, in competitive and non-competitive areas. They are a useful benchmark, not a quote for your meter, and they include energy and delivery.

Sources: EIA Electric Power Annual Table 2.10 and EIA Electric Power Monthly Table 5.6.A (September 2026 data released November 24, 2026).

Is electricity deregulated in Texas? What about Dallas, Fort Worth, Garland, or Denton?

In much of Texas, including most of Dallas–Fort Worth served by Oncor (and some pockets served by TNMP), businesses can choose a retail electric provider. The local utility still delivers the power. Many municipal utilities and electric cooperatives are not open to choice, including Garland Power & Light and Denton Municipal Electric, so those businesses generally cannot shop providers. Confirm from your bill or ESI ID. City name and ZIP code alone can be misleading because utility boundaries cross city lines. Retail choice for eligible customers began January 1, 2002.

Sources: PUCT 2003 Scope of Competition and Power to Choose, Business (Non-residential).

Can I use Power to Choose for my business?

Power to Choose (powertochoose.org) is the official PUCT shopping site, but it is built around residential plans. The site itself says shopping for business service is often a negotiated purchase, more like buying equipment in bulk than picking a listed home plan. It publishes a directory of non-residential retail electric providers, not a commercial rate board. For most businesses, a bill review and broker or direct-provider quotes are the practical way to compare.

Sources: Power to Choose, Business (Non-residential) and the non-residential provider directory.

How do I compare commercial electricity rates in Texas?

Start with 12 months of bills if you have them, plus one recent bill that shows your ESI ID, utility (TDU), usage, and demand. Compare offers on the same term and the same all-in picture: energy rate plus the utility's delivery charges, not just the lowest advertised cents per kWh. We can do that review for you from a bill upload.

Can I switch commercial electricity providers? Will my power go out?

If your address is in a competitive area and your current contract allows it (or you accept any early-exit terms), yes. A normal switch changes the retail electric provider only. The same utility still delivers the power, and the meter should not be disconnected as part of a standard switch. If you are still in a fixed term, ask your current provider for a written early-termination calculation before you sign a new deal.

What happens when my commercial electricity contract expires?

Service usually continues, but the negotiated fixed rate often ends. Many contracts then move the account to a month-to-month, holdover, or variable rate, which can be substantially higher. Some contracts auto-renew into a new term instead. Read your agreement and start comparing options before the end date, not after.

What is the difference between a fixed rate and a variable rate?

A fixed-rate plan keeps the energy rate the same through the contract term, which makes budgeting easier. A variable rate can move with the market and may let you leave sooner, but the bill can jump. Delivery charges from your utility are separate and can still change under the regulated tariff.

Why is my business electric bill so high?

It may be the energy rate, a contract that expired, demand charges based on your peak kW, a low power factor, sales tax that should have been exempt, or simply higher usage. The only honest way to tell is to read the bill, not to guess a savings number. Send us a recent bill and we will tell you what we see.
