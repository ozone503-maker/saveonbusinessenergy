Summer in Texas: why your bill spikes

Every Texas business knows the summer bill. It arrives bigger than the spring one, and nobody changed anything. Here is why it happens, and what to do before the heat hits.

Your summer bill spikes because everything runs at once — more hours, more cooling, higher peak demand — even on a fixed rate.
Key stat: ERCOT forecast a summer 2026 peak of 92,211 MW, about 8% above the August 2023 record of about 85,500 MW. Summer peak demand rose from 71,110 MW in 2016 to 91,134 MW in 2026.
Source: ERCOT, June 2026, via Energy Ogre Q2 2026 market update; ERCOT 2025 State of the Grid report.

Why summer costs more

Cooling runs longer

Air conditioning is the biggest summer load for most businesses. It runs more hours, and it runs harder. More hours means more kilowatt-hours.

Peak demand climbs

Cooling and equipment peak together on hot afternoons. That pushes your peak kilowatts up, and peak demand is a large part of the delivery bill for many commercial meters.

One hot afternoon can set your demand charge for the month.

Why a fixed rate does not fix it

Rate vs. usage

A fixed rate fixes the energy rate, not the whole bill. Your usage changes month to month. Summer usage is higher, so the bill is higher.

Demand charges

Demand is separate from the energy rate a provider quotes. A fixed rate does nothing about a demand spike.

Before the heat hits

  • Check your contract end date before summer, not during it.
  • Stagger the big loads so they do not all peak on the same hot afternoon.
  • Check whether power factor is quietly raising your bill.
  • Review the bill, not just the rate.

FAQ

Will my rate go up in summer?

A fixed rate stays fixed. The bill rises because usage and demand rise. If your rate is variable, the rate itself can move too.

When should I review before summer?

Before the fixed term ends. Do not wait for the spike to find out your contract already rolled onto a holdover rate.
